# Write posts that sound like you

Most engineers don't struggle with ideas. They struggle with which idea to commit to, how to shape it, and how often to post. This kit settles all three, then gives you prompts that make an AI draft sound like you instead of like everyone else.

## One post, one observation

A post is one thing you noticed, from one piece of real work, said plainly. Not a summary of your week and not a lesson for everyone. If you can't say the observation in one sentence, you aren't ready to write the post yet.

Good sources, in order of strength: something you built and what it changed; a decision you made and why; a failure and the fix; something you noticed in someone else's product; a question your work raised that you can't answer yet.

## Four shapes that work

Pick the shape after you have the observation. Each one fits in 80 to 200 words.

**1. Noticed → why it matters → question.** Open with the concrete thing you saw. Say what it suggests about how software gets built now. End with a real question you'd like answered.

**2. Problem → decision → result.** Name the problem in the user's words. Say the one decision you made and the alternative you rejected. Give the result, with a number if you have one.

**3. Build log with a visual.** A screenshot, diagram or short clip, plus three lines: what the screen shows, the decision behind it, and why it matters to the person using it. The visual is never the whole post.

**4. Comment, grown up.** Take your best comment from this week, the one that started a conversation, and expand it into a post that credits the original idea.

## Cadence

Start at two posts a week and three substantive comments a day. Draft in one sitting on the weekend, then post on two weekday mornings. Keep a running note of observations as they happen: when you have five, you have a week. Increase only if you're still noticing new things without forcing it.

## Make an AI draft sound like you

AI can shape a draft quickly, but left alone it writes like nobody in particular. Give it your voice first, then use it to edit, never to invent.

**Step 1: build your voice profile (once).** Paste three to five things you've written yourself: messages, emails, old posts, comments. Then use this prompt:

> Here are some things I wrote. Describe how I write: sentence length, word choice, how formal I am, how I open and close, what I never say. Write it as a short list I can reuse as instructions. Don't flatter me, and don't add traits that aren't in the samples.

Save the result. That's your voice file.

**Step 2: draft from your notes, in your voice.** Use this prompt with your observation and any facts:

> Using my voice file below, turn these notes into a LinkedIn post under 180 words using the "[shape name]" shape. Use only facts in my notes. Keep my words where you can. No emojis, no hashtags, no "here's the thing", no lists unless my notes have one. End with one real question.
> Voice file: [paste]
> Notes: [paste]

**Step 3: tighten it.**

> Cut this post by a third without losing the observation. Make the first line something a busy engineer would stop for, without exaggerating. Point out any sentence that sounds generic or that I didn't actually say in my notes.

**Step 4: check every claim.** Read the draft against what really happened. Remove anything you can't show or explain in an interview. Then rewrite at least one sentence in your own words before you post, so the voice stays yours as it changes.

## Before you post

- One observation, said in the first two lines.
- Every claim is something you did, saw or can show.
- The visual, if any, is explained.
- It ends with something a reader can answer.
- You'd be comfortable if a hiring manager read it tomorrow.
