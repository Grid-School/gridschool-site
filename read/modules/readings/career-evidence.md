# Help the right people see your work

Your job search needs three separate checks: whether a role is open to you, whether someone sees your application, and whether your work answers their concerns. A stronger project cannot remove a work-authorization requirement. A popular post cannot answer a debugging question. Review all three with Aden before increasing the volume of applications.

## Start before you feel ready to perform

In week 1, save your current resume and LinkedIn profile and choose two role families with Aden. Read five current postings for each. Record recurring skills, location, authorization, experience requirements, and any stated AI policy for interviews. Identify one gap that would prevent you from doing a small task in those roles.

Practice visibility in steps. First explain a bug privately to Aden. Then share a screenshot and explanation with the cohort. After your first reviewed change, publish one explanation that names exactly what you did. A screen recording with captions can show the work without putting your face on camera. Public claims should describe practice as practice and education as education.

By week 2, revise your profile and draft a resume from existing evidence. In week 4, review the resume and practice an interview. These coaching sessions happen even if a later Career step on the map is still closed.

## Check readiness for a particular role

You can begin applications when you can explain one reviewed change, reproduce its check, identify a limitation, debug a small unfamiliar variation, and accurately describe your part in the work. Review that evidence with Aden against the role. You can continue applying while you strengthen other skills. You do not need to finish every course step first.

Practice the interview the employer actually uses: a small coding problem with edge cases and complexity, reading unfamiliar code, a SQL query, or a system discussion. Use AI only when the interview permits it. Each week, spend one focused practice session on the weakest relevant format and revisit a changed version later.

## A weekly routine during the intensive

Reserve two hours a week for career work, including drafting, conversation, applications, and interview practice. The profile, resume, and demo tasks count toward that time. Begin with two useful posts a week after you have reviewed work to discuss; increase to three only if the work supports it. Spend two short sessions reading and commenting on relevant engineers' or recruiters' posts. Write three to five substantive comments per session and up to five relevant connection invitations per week. These starting budgets support practice. They are neither platform limits nor proven hiring thresholds.

A useful comment adds an observation, reproducible example, respectful correction with a source, or a specific question you have considered. Read the post first. If you have nothing useful to add, move on. A list of 20 engineers, alumni, maintainers, hiring managers, and recruiters in your target area will serve you better than an endless feed.

## The ten-day visibility experiment

After the intensive, you and Aden can test the proposed higher-volume routine for **10 working days: five posts and 20 substantive comments per day**. This is an experiment, and it has no promised time to interview or hire. Begin only when you have a defensible project, a reviewed profile, relevant people to learn from, and enough distinct observations to prepare the first three days of posts.

Use five different artifacts from the week's work: a demo, a failure and repair, a diagram, a measured result, and a decision or lesson. You may reuse a project, but each post must add a new observation. Prepare drafts in batches. Read every draft, check its claims against the source, and publish deliberately. AI can help edit the wording; you remain responsible for the facts and every conversation.

Cap the entire routine, including preparation, at two hours per working day. Attempt up to ten personalized invitations to relevant recruiters or engineers per day within that time. Select recruiters with a current role that fits your eligibility and evidence. Stop when LinkedIn signals a restriction. These invitation numbers belong to the course experiment. LinkedIn's limits can differ. Never use automated comments, invitation bots, purchased engagement, or coordinated likes.

Record a baseline over the preceding two weeks using the same definitions. Review the experiment on days 5 and 10 and again two weeks afterward. Reduce the cadence if you run out of useful observations, miss engineering or interview work, exceed the time cap, or receive negative feedback about repetition. Five low-value posts do not satisfy the exercise. At day 10, keep only the parts that produced useful conversations at a sustainable cost.

## What to measure

Use one private table with date, artifact or role URL, channel, minutes, action, response, and next step. Record applications and conversations separately so the same person is not counted twice. For each channel, report:

- Invitations sent and accepted; acceptance rate is accepted divided by sent.
- Substantive replies: a person discussed the work, gave feedback, offered a relevant conversation, or named an opportunity. Reactions and automated replies do not count.
- Applications submitted, recruiter screens, technical interviews, and offers, with dates and role eligibility.
- Hours spent and how many relevant conversations those hours produced. When there are no conversations, report zero conversations rather than dividing by zero.

Track impressions as context. They are not interviews. At each review, inspect one unsuccessful application and one conversation. If applications get no screens, inspect eligibility, resume clarity, and channels. If screens do not progress, inspect your examples and role fit. If technical rounds fail, practice the specific skill the round exposed. Small samples guide the next test; they cannot establish that posting caused an offer.

## Formats you can fill in

Replace every bracket with a fact you can support. Remove a sentence if you do not have the evidence. Add the artifact link directly where a reader can find it. Keep private names, customer data, tokens, and internal URLs out of public captures.

### A demo post

> I built [small change] for [user or role] because [observed problem]. This clip shows [input] becoming [result]. I checked [failure case] with [test]. [Known limitation] still needs work. Code and explanation: [URL].

Attach a 15-to-30-second screen capture or looping GIF of one workflow. Add a still image and a plain-language caption so the result is understandable without motion. Show a real run; label fixtures and staged failures.

### A failure post

> I expected [behavior]. With [specific input], the system did [observed behavior]. The cause was [supported explanation]. I changed [small part] and added [check]. The check covers [scope]; it does not cover [remaining case]. Reproduction and change: [URL].

Attach a readable before-and-after result or one annotated screenshot. The caption should name the input and the result.

### A measurement post

> In [number] comparable trials over [dates], [metric with unit] changed from [baseline] to [result], including [review or correction time]. I calculated the change as [formula]. [Confound or sample limitation] means I can only conclude [narrow conclusion]. Method and data: [URL].

Use a labeled chart or table with sample size, units, and dates. Distinguish measurements you collected from numbers reported by another source. Link the original source and its date for an external statistic.

### A design decision post

> For [requirement], I considered [two options]. I chose [option] because [constraint]. The cost is [tradeoff]. If [condition changes], I would revisit it. Diagram, implementation, and check: [URL].

### A useful comment

> I tried [related case] and observed [result]. Your point about [specific part] makes me wonder whether [testable condition] changes the result. Here is [brief example or source] if useful.

Use only clauses you can honestly fill in. A specific question can stand alone. Avoid adding a project link when it does not help the conversation.

### A recruiter invitation

> Hi [name], I saw you recruit for [role or team]. I am looking for [eligible role/location] and recently worked on [specific relevant change]. I would like to follow the roles you share.

After acceptance, if a suitable role exists:

> Thanks for connecting. For [role URL], my closest example is [artifact URL]: I [contribution] and checked [behavior]. Are you the right person to ask about [one specific requirement or next step]?

Send one short follow-up after about a week when appropriate, then move on. Ask an engineer for a referral only after they have enough context to decide whether they can honestly recommend your work.

## A 90-second demo script

| Time | Picture | Say |
|---|---|---|
| 0–15 seconds | The original workflow | “[Person or role] needs to [task]. Previously, [observed problem].” |
| 15–40 seconds | One real input and output | “Here is [input]. The system [action]. The user checks [decision] before [consequence].” |
| 40–60 seconds | A failing case and its check | “With [bad input], the system [safe behavior]. This check would catch [specific failure].” |
| 60–80 seconds | Measured result with units | “Across [sample], I observed [result]. [Limitation] keeps the conclusion narrow.” |
| 80–90 seconds | Repository or project page | “I built [your contribution]. The code, checks, and limitations are at [URL].” |

For the required three-to-six-minute demo, use the same order and expand the request trace, one decision, and the reproduction of the failure. Keep a private practice recording, get one round of feedback, then record again. Captions and a transcript make the explanation easier to inspect.

## Use several routes into the market

Apply to eligible jobs on the employer's own site and use university career services, alumni conversations, local employers, and relevant communities. Investigate internships, apprenticeships, QA automation, support engineering, and implementation roles when they match your interests and eligibility. A paid role with supervision can be useful experience; ask what work you will actually do and who will review it. Do not describe GridSchool as employment or count a course exercise as commercial production experience.

Research supports practicing demonstrable skills and building professional relationships. It does not establish a posting quota that produces offers. NACE's 2026 employer survey reports that 70% of respondents use skills-based hiring; that sample is not all employers or specifically junior software teams. A large randomized LinkedIn study found that some weaker professional connections helped job mobility, with differences across industries. Neither study tests this course's posting routine. [NACE survey](https://naceweb.org/job-market/trends-and-predictions/employer-use-of-skills-based-hiring-practices-grows) · [LinkedIn network experiment](https://ide.mit.edu/wp-content/uploads/2022/09/abl4476.pdf).

**Done when** your profile points to work you can explain, your weekly plan fits your available time, and your private table records the next action for each relevant conversation or application.
